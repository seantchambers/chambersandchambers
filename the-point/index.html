Point

A point has a position and nothing else. Every drawing starts with one, and so does every building.

Euclid opens the Elements with it: a point is that which has no part. It has a location but no length, width or depth. Strictly, you can't draw one. Any mark a pen leaves already covers a small area. On a plotter the point is the simplest instruction there is: move, lower the pen, lift it.

In practice the point is where everything is set out from. A survey benchmark, a grid intersection, the setting-out point at a portal knee where the column and rafter centrelines cross. In plan a column is a point, and a field of them is the first sketch of a building. Take the walls away and the points are what's left holding it up.

Silence and speech

Kandinsky starts in the same place. Point and Line to Plane, published in 1926 as the ninth Bauhaus book, treats the point as the most concise form there is, as close to saying nothing as a mark can get while still saying something. He describes it as the meeting of silence and speech. Then he moves it. A point at the centre of a square sits still. Move it off-centre and the whole square starts to lean.

Many points

Seurat painted A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884–86) in small touches of unmixed colour and left the mixing to the eye. Halftone printing does the same with ink. The old stipple engravings in anatomy and botany books do it with a pen. In all of them, tone is density: the number of points in a given area.

Xenakis brought the idea into music. In Pithoprakta (1955–56) he gave 46 string players separate parts and treated them like molecules in a gas. Each part follows the statistics of molecular motion, so no single line goes anywhere, but the mass drifts and heats and cools. A few years later, in Analogique B, he built a piece from tiny grains of sound, an early version of what is now called granular synthesis. The note becomes a point, and the sound becomes a cloud of them.
