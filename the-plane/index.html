The plane

A plane is a line that has moved sideways. It is where a drawing first starts to enclose something, and where a building first meets the light.

In Ching's Form, Space and Order a line extended in any direction other than its own becomes a plane. It has length and width but no depth, and its character comes from its shape, its surface and which way it faces. Kandinsky's book ends here too, at what he calls the basic plane: the surface that receives the work. He doesn't think it is neutral. The top of a sheet feels light and loose, the bottom heavy, and the edges pull.

A plotter cannot draw a plane at all. It can draw the edges, and it can fill the area with lines. Every surface in the drawings on this page is a field of hatching. Tone is spacing.

Ching gives architecture three planes: the base plane we stand on, the wall planes that divide and enclose, and the overhead plane that shelters. On a drawing, the slab sits at a reduced level, the site is a stack of contours, and every section is a cutting plane. Poché, the dark fill where walls are cut, is how the drawing tells you a plane has been sliced through.

Planes that slide apart

De Stijl took the box apart. At the Rietveld Schröder House in Utrecht (1924), walls, roofs and balconies are separate planes that slip past each other and stop short at the corners. In the same year Mies drew his Brick Country House as a scatter of free-standing walls reaching out into the landscape, with no rooms in the usual sense. The Barcelona Pavilion (1929) built the idea: wall planes of stone and glass under a flat roof plane carried on slender columns, with space running between them instead of being shut inside.

Light

A plane is how a building catches light. Turn a wall a few degrees and its brightness changes. Raking light across a rendered wall will find every flaw in it. Tadao Ando's Church of the Light in Ibaraki (1989) is close to a single idea: a concrete wall behind the altar, cut through by a cross that runs its full height and width, so the light seems to float on the plane. James Turrell's skyspaces work the other way. A crisp opening in an overhead plane frames the sky so tightly that it seems to sit on the ceiling, close enough to touch.

A plane of sound

Ligeti's Atmosphères (1961) does with an orchestra what hatching does with a pen. Each string player has an individual part, but the entries are so close together that no line can be heard. What reaches you is a surface of sound that brightens, darkens and slides, with no melody or beat to hold on to. Ligeti called the counterpoint underwater: it is there, but you only hear the plane it makes.
