The curve

A pen plotter can only draw straight lines. Every curve it makes is assembled from them. So, it turns out, is a surprising amount of architecture.

Ask a plotter for a circle and its firmware quietly breaks the arc into short chords, each a straight move of the pen. Make the chords short enough and the corners between them fall below what the eye can see. The curve is something we assemble afterwards.

This is an old trick. Archimedes pinned down π by fitting polygons inside and outside a circle and doubling their sides until he reached 96. No curved line was measured. He caged the circle between straight edges and read the answer from the gap.

Forty-six strings and a pavilion

Iannis Xenakis trained as an engineer and worked for Le Corbusier through the 1950s while teaching himself to compose. For Metastaseis (1953–54) he gave each of 46 string players a separate part and drew the score on graph paper: pitch up the side, time along the bottom, each player's glissando a single straight line. Together the lines produced curves that no single line contained.

When the design of the Philips Pavilion for the 1958 Brussels World's Fair came to him, he built the same geometry in concrete. A hyperbolic paraboloid is curved in two directions, yet two families of straight lines lie across its surface. That made it buildable: the shells were cast as small precast panels following the straight rulings. The music and the building answered one question. How do you move from one point to another without a break?

Gravel

In 1968 Georg Nees drew Schotter on a Zuse Graphomat, a flatbed plotter fed by punched tape: rows of squares that begin in order and fall into disorder down the sheet. A few years later he was plotting roof trusses with the architect Ludwig Rase. Plotters and buildings have shared a room for a long time.

Xenakis came back to the graph paper in 1977 with UPIC, a drawing tablet that played what you drew. Time ran left to right, pitch up the page. The score had become the instrument.
